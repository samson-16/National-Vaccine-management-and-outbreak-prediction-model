# Public Data Strategy for Woreda Level Measles Outbreak Prediction in Ethiopia

## Feasibility and the strongest project framing

Your project is feasible, but the strongest **public-data MVP** is not a fully supervised, all-Ethiopia woreda model built from a clean national measles line list. I could not verify a fully open national woreda-level measles case line list for Ethiopia. The best public national sources I found are a WHO AFRO monthly measles report built from **district-level DHIS2 summaries**, Ethiopia’s weekly epidemiological bulletins and outbreak notices, and a published method paper that estimated **MCV1 coverage for Ethiopian woredas** using DHIS2, EDHS, and population data; that paper explicitly notes that raw DHIS2 data require permission from the Ethiopian Ministry of Health. citeturn42view0turn28view0turn29view0turn35view0

The most important finding for your graduation project is that **public woreda-level measles data do exist for at least one major Ethiopian region**. The Amhara Public Health Institute research data repository lists public measles datasets that include woreda fields, including a **measles line list for September 2023 to April 2025** with 6,161 observations and variables such as region, zone, woreda, kebele, age, symptoms, onset date, vaccination doses, specimen status, complications, treatment, and outcome. The same repository also lists a second longer **Amhara measles line list for August 2022 to June 2025**, and aggregate **measles cases and deaths** datasets with woreda/hospital, month, epi-week, population, and measles case/death variables. citeturn25search5turn27search1turn25search1turn27search11

That means the most defensible framing is this: build an **MVP supervised forecasting model in Amhara** using public measles outcomes, then build a **national woreda-level risk model** for Ethiopia using public covariates and weaker national outbreak labels until your own platform accumulates better labels. This is academically sound because Ethiopia has had a large ongoing measles burden, with EPHI reporting **78,106 measles cases and 633 deaths in 458 woredas nationwide between August 2021 and December 2024**, while WHO reported active outbreaks in **44 woredas** as of May 2023 and linked spread to low immunity, conflict, displacement, malnutrition, weak surveillance, and access barriers. citeturn34view0turn35view0

For the actual prediction target, the best unit is **woreda-by-week** or **woreda-by-month**, not daily prediction. That matches how Ethiopian and WHO surveillance products are summarized, and it aligns with WHO measles outbreak definitions. WHO’s measles toolbox defines a suspected outbreak as **five or more epidemiologically linked cases**, and WHO AFRO guidance defines a suspected outbreak as **five or more suspected cases in a district in one month**, with a confirmed outbreak at **three or more IgM-positive cases in a district in one month**. WHO’s Ethiopia outbreak page also uses “active outbreak” as **three or more measles IgM-positive cases within one month**. citeturn1search0turn1search4turn35view0

## Attributes that matter most for the model

The most important feature family is **population immunity and susceptible accumulation**. Measles transmission stops only when population immunity is very high; WHO states that roughly **95% coverage** with measles-containing vaccine is needed to interrupt circulation. In Ethiopia, low MCV1 and MCV2 coverage, low population immunity, and disrupted vaccination during conflict and humanitarian crises have been repeatedly linked to outbreaks. A 2024 systematic review on recurrent measles outbreaks in Ethiopia identified low routine coverage, weak SIAs, poor nutrition, limited access to care, contact with cases, seasonal weather patterns, inadequate surveillance, and inequality as key drivers. citeturn35view0turn20search0turn1search15

So your core immunity features should include **MCV1 coverage, MCV2 coverage or a proxy for it, measles dropout rate, zero-dose prevalence, time since last campaign, births, infant population, and cumulative “missed vaccination” pressure**. That last idea matters because measles risk grows when the susceptible pool is replenished by births faster than the vaccination system closes the gap. Ethiopia’s own district-level immunization study used DHIS2 vaccination counts together with DHIS2, CSA, and WorldPop denominators to estimate **MCV1 coverage across all Ethiopian woredas**, precisely because local susceptibility is what matters operationally. citeturn28view0turn29view1

The next feature family is **contact and mixing intensity**. Measles spreads through close human contact, and classic work on measles seasonality shows that transmission is shaped by school-term aggregation, seasonal dry-season peaks in tropical Africa, and seasonal population flux. In West Africa, seasonal changes in population density measured through night-time lights were shown to track measles seasonality; school vacations have also been associated with clear reductions in transmission. That means your model should include **population density, urbanization, settlement density, school-age population share, night-light intensity, night-light seasonality, and movement/displacement proxies**. citeturn33search0turn33search1turn33search3turn0search10

The third feature family is **health service access and delivery quality**. WHO’s Ethiopia outbreak assessment explicitly names weak surveillance, late detection, inadequate outbreak investigation, poor transport, poor roads, and difficulty delivering supplies as reasons the national risk remained high. Ethiopian vaccination analyses also show that dropout and under-coverage are strongly associated with **rural residence, antenatal care utilization, maternal social conditions, and region-specific inequalities**. A 2024 study on measles vaccination dropout in Ethiopia found higher dropout odds for short birth interval, rural residence, and low community ANC use; a separate Ethiopian MCV2 dropout study identified maternal illiteracy, lack of postnatal care, poor counseling, long travel distance, and long waiting time as key determinants. citeturn35view0turn44view0turn19search7turn19search15

The fourth feature family is **shock and vulnerability context**. WHO’s Ethiopia outbreak page directly links outbreak risk to conflict, forced displacement, drought, flooding, malnutrition, crowding, and poor sanitation. Ethiopian outbreak investigations also repeatedly identify non-vaccination, travel to affected areas, contact with cases, and malnutrition as outbreak-associated factors. For a woreda model, this means you should include **conflict events, humanitarian access level, IDP/refugee presence, drought/flood anomalies, rainfall, temperature, food insecurity, market stress, and child malnutrition proxies**. citeturn35view0turn17search5turn17search11turn20search0

Finally, the feature that often matters most in practice is **recent local and neighboring measles activity**. If you can produce any public or semi-public outbreak label at woreda level, the model should include **lagged cases, lagged outbreak status, neighboring-woreda lag, and regional incidence**. Ethiopia’s surveillance and response guidelines emphasize analyzing outbreak data by **time, place, and person**, which is exactly the logic behind those lagged spatial-temporal predictors. citeturn10search10turn35view0

## Public datasets you can actually use

The most useful public datasets for your MVP are the following.

**Public measles outcome data for supervised modeling.**  
The strongest verified source is the **Amhara Public Health Institute RDMC**. It lists a measles line list for **September 2023 to April 2025** with woreda and kebele fields plus epidemiologically useful variables, and another line list from **August 2022 to June 2025**. It also lists aggregate Amhara measles burden datasets, including one for **July 2024 to June 2025** with woreda/hospital, population, year, quarter, month, epi-week, and measles case/inpatient/death variables, and a longer historical Amhara measles cases-and-deaths dataset covering **July 2015 to June 2024**. For an MVP, this is enough to build a real woreda-level supervised model in one region. citeturn25search5turn27search1turn25search1turn27search11turn25search12

**Public outbreak and weak-label data for national Ethiopia.**  
WHO’s Disease Outbreak News for Ethiopia provides nationally curated outbreak intelligence, including counts of affected woredas, regional spread, risk factors, and campaign response. EPHI’s weekly epidemiological bulletins regularly publish suspected measles summaries. WHO AFRO’s public monthly measles data pack is based on **district-level DHIS2 summaries** and includes Ethiopia-specific age, gender, and vaccination trend panels. This is weaker than a national line list, but it is enough to create **weak labels**, for example “woreda-month likely outbreak” when a woreda appears in public outbreak notices or aggregate surveillance thresholds. citeturn35view0turn12search1turn42view0

**Vaccination coverage and susceptibility data.**  
A 2024 PLOS Global Public Health study estimated **MCV1 coverage at woreda level across Ethiopia** using DHIS2 numerator data and WorldPop, CSA, and DHIS2 denominators, adjusted against EDHS. The paper reports results for **1,031 woredas** and is one of the best countrywide public susceptibility layers available. In addition, the APHI repository lists a public **routine immunization coverage and vaccine utilization dataset from 2018 to 2024** by organization unit and time period, including doses given and vaccine management variables such as doses opened, damaged, and expired. DHS also provides Ethiopia cluster-level indicator data, including measles vaccination indicators, and a geospatial covariates system that links clusters to population, climate, infrastructure, and environmental factors. citeturn28view0turn29view1turn22view0turn2search0turn13search4turn13search1

**Population, births, and age structure.**  
WorldPop provides Ethiopia raster layers for **total population**, **age-sex structure**, and **births**, including 100 m age-structured population surfaces and births datasets that can be aggregated to woreda boundaries. These are ideal for features like under-one population, under-five population, school-age population, births, and density per square kilometer. citeturn32search11turn32search4turn32search1turn32search8

**Administrative boundaries.**  
For woreda geometry, use a single canonical boundary set and keep it fixed across the whole project. HDX’s Ethiopia COD-AB dataset lists **Admin 3: 1,148 woredas**, while other analytical datasets use slightly different counts because of timing and administrative change. The PLOS woreda coverage paper also had to probabilistically match woreda names across DHIS2, WorldPop, and CSA because names and boundaries were not standardized. Boundary harmonization is therefore not a minor issue; it is one of the main technical risks in your project. citeturn36search16turn29view1

**Access to care and service geography.**  
The Malaria Atlas Project provides downloadable **1 km travel-time-to-healthcare** layers for walking-only and motorized access, which are very strong proxies for vaccination access and case management access. Healthsites.io provides an open, downloadable shapefile-based health facility inventory, and HDX also lists Ethiopia health facility location datasets. These let you compute facility density, nearest-facility distance, and reporting catchment proxies by woreda. citeturn39view0turn38view0turn6search6

**Climate and environmental drivers.**  
CHIRPS provides rainfall from **1981 to near-present** at **0.05°** resolution, and ERA5-Land provides hourly or monthly land-surface variables from **1950 to near-present** at roughly **9 km** resolution. These are enough to derive rainfall anomalies, dry-season indicators, temperature, humidity proxies, and lagged environmental signals. citeturn5search0turn5search17turn5search1

**Conflict, displacement, food insecurity, and market stress.**  
ACLED provides geocoded Ethiopia conflict event data, which can be spatially joined to woreda boundaries. IOM’s DTM Ethiopia work tracks displacement and explicitly reports data at **zone, woreda, and site level** in its mobility tracking products. OCHA publishes **woreda-level humanitarian access** analysis for Ethiopia. FEWS NET publishes Ethiopia **weekly staple food price data** and geographic **acute food insecurity classifications**, both valuable for vulnerability and humanitarian stress features. citeturn31search15turn31search2turn7search24turn36search2turn9search0turn9search1

Where data are missing, use **proxies**, not invented labels. If MCV2 coverage is missing, proxy it with MCV1, measles dropout, ANC utilization, maternal education, travel time, and waiting-time/access constraints. If direct mobility data are missing, use displacement flows, humanitarian access, road/travel-time layers, and seasonal variation in night-time lights. If cold-chain data are missing, use vaccine management variables such as opened, damaged, and expired doses from the EPI dataset plus facility density and access indicators. What you should **not** do is generate fake measles outcome labels and then report predictive performance on those synthetic labels as if they were real. citeturn19search7turn33search0turn22view0turn39view0

## A realistic MVP design

The best MVP has **two layers**.

The first layer is a **real supervised forecasting model in Amhara**. Aggregate the public APHI line list to **woreda-week** or **woreda-month** and create two targets: a count target such as measles cases in the next period, and a binary target such as whether the woreda exceeds a suspected or confirmed outbreak threshold in the next 4 weeks. Add lagged cases, vaccination pressure variables, rainfall/temperature, travel access, population density, and conflict/displacement variables. This gives you a genuine predictive ML result with public disease labels. citeturn25search5turn25search1turn1search4turn35view0

The second layer is a **national Ethiopia risk model**, not a national exact-case model. Build a woreda-level “risk of outbreak soon” score for the whole country using the national public covariates: woreda MCV1 estimates, DHS-derived dropout/zero-dose proxies, WorldPop births and child population, CHIRPS and ERA5-Land climate signals, MAP travel time, health facility density, conflict/displacement layers, and recent public outbreak information from WHO/EPHI. If you can extract weak public outbreak labels from EPHI/WHO notices, you can train a weakly supervised classifier; if not, frame the output as a calibrated **risk ranking** rather than a precise forecast. citeturn28view0turn44view0turn32search11turn5search0turn39view0turn35view0

For models, start simple and rigorous. Use a **baseline logistic regression** for outbreak/no-outbreak and a **baseline negative binomial or Poisson model** for counts. Then compare them with one tree-based model such as **XGBoost or LightGBM**. If you have enough observations, add a spatial model with neighboring-woreda lag or spatial random effects. Your innovation is not just “using a complex algorithm”; it is building a **woreda-linked, public-data, spatiotemporal measles early-warning pipeline** under real Ethiopian data constraints. That is already a strong final-year contribution. citeturn35view0turn29view1

Validation matters more than model complexity. Use **time-based splits** so the model predicts future periods rather than memorizing the past. Also do a **spatial holdout**, where some woredas are kept completely out of training, because overfitting to place is very common in geospatial health models. Report **ROC-AUC and PR-AUC** for outbreak classification, and **MAE/RMSE** for count prediction if you include count targets. Also report **Brier score or calibration plots**, because outbreak models are often more useful when the probabilities are well calibrated than when the raw AUC is slightly higher. citeturn1search0turn35view0

## What the ML section of your paper should contain

The ML section of the paper should read like a serious epidemiological modeling study, not just a software demo.

Start by defining the **research question** clearly: for example, “Can public covariates and public surveillance signals predict measles outbreak risk at woreda level in Ethiopia or, for the supervised region-specific MVP, in Amhara?” Then define the **unit of analysis** as woreda-week or woreda-month and justify it using surveillance practice and outbreak thresholds. citeturn1search4turn35view0turn42view0

Then describe the **target variable** precisely. If you use Amhara line-list data, explain how you aggregated case records to woreda-time units and how you defined future case counts or future outbreak status. If you use a national risk model, explain whether the label is weakly supervised from public outbreak notices or whether the model is explicitly a risk-ranking model rather than a direct outcome predictor. This distinction is academically important and should be stated transparently. citeturn25search5turn35view0

The next part should document **data sources and linkage**. Explain how you linked APHI measles data, boundaries, WorldPop rasters, climate rasters, access layers, conflict/displacement data, and vaccination estimates to the woreda level. Mention boundary and spelling mismatches explicitly, because Ethiopian woreda naming and administrative changes are a known technical problem and were important enough that the district immunization study had to use probabilistic name matching across datasets. citeturn29view1turn36search16

After that, include **data quality and preprocessing**. Report missingness, outliers, reporting gaps, harmonization steps, temporal aggregation, geospatial joins, and any imputation. The district-level immunization paper is a good model for this because it explicitly checked completeness, outliers, internal consistency, and time consistency in DHIS2-derived data. If you do not write this part carefully, your paper will look weak even if the model is good. citeturn29view1

Your **feature engineering** subsection should separate immunity features, demographics, service access, climate/shock features, and spatiotemporal lag features. Then in the **modeling** subsection, compare at least one transparent baseline and one stronger model. Do not skip the baseline. A graduation paper is much stronger when it shows that a simple model, a stronger model, and a hybrid or spatial version were compared fairly. citeturn20search0turn35view0

Your **evaluation** subsection should include discrimination, calibration, and practical interpretability. Add SHAP or another explainability method if you use tree models, but also show sensible epidemiological interpretation: if vaccination gap, births, displacement, travel time, and lagged outbreaks rank highly, that strengthens the scientific credibility of the work. End with **ethics and limitations**: national labels are incomplete, some public datasets are regional, some systems require permission, and surveillance under conflict can undercount cases. Saying this clearly improves the paper; hiding it weakens it. citeturn35view0turn29view0turn31search15

## What your data platform should collect

Because your platform is part of the project, it should be designed to create the **gold-standard dataset you do not currently have**.

At the **case level**, collect the variables that already appear in the public Amhara line-list structure: case ID, suspected/confirmed status, woreda, kebele, facility, age, sex, date of symptom onset, date seen at facility, symptoms, vaccination doses received, specimen taken, lab result if available, complication, treatment, hospitalization, and outcome. Those fields are directly useful for both surveillance and future supervised learning. Ethiopia’s outbreak guidance also emphasizes organizing measles data by **time, place, and person**, which fits this design exactly. citeturn25search5turn10search10

At the **facility-week** level, collect immunization service data: MCV1 doses given, MCV2 doses given, outreach sessions planned and completed, stock on hand, doses opened, doses damaged, doses expired, stockout days, fridge downtime or temperature excursions if possible, and reporting completeness. The public APHI EPI dataset already shows the value of storing vaccine utilization variables, not only vaccination counts. These variables are crucial because measles outbreaks often reflect service-delivery breakdown as much as they reflect population biology. citeturn22view0turn35view0

At the **woreda-week** level, collect contextual operational variables: local campaign dates, school-opening periods if available, IDP arrivals or camp expansion, major access interruptions, flooding or drought incidents, and whether neighboring woredas reported outbreaks. WHO’s Ethiopia risk assessment explicitly identifies conflict, movement, poor roads, drought, floods, weak surveillance, and supply-access problems as outbreak-relevant conditions, so storing those variables prospectively will make later models much better than anything you can build from retrospective public data alone. citeturn35view0

The best way to use platform data over time is threefold. First, use it to create **true woreda labels** for outbreak/no-outbreak and case counts. Second, use it to update a **susceptible-pool view** through vaccination coverage, births, and missed children. Third, use it for **continual learning**: retrain or recalibrate the model monthly or quarterly and compare performance across seasons, regions, and conflict conditions. This turns your project from a one-time prediction demo into a deployable decision-support system. citeturn42view0turn22view0turn35view0

## If ML accuracy is weak

If direct ML performance is weak, your project can still be excellent by moving toward a **hybrid early-warning system**.

The first alternative is a **rule-based susceptibility and threshold model**. Use WHO outbreak thresholds together with a woreda susceptibility score constructed from births, under-one population, MCV1/MCV2 shortfall, time since campaign, and recent neighboring transmission. If the susceptibility score is high and recent incidence or neighboring incidence rises, flag the woreda. This is much easier to explain to examiners and public health officers than a black-box forecast, and it maps directly to how measles response is operationalized. citeturn1search0turn1search4turn35view0

The second alternative is a **mechanistic transmission model**, especially a TSIR or state-space SEIR-type approach. Measles has a strong tradition of mechanistic modeling because susceptible accumulation from births and imperfect vaccination is central to epidemic timing. There are published measles state-space approaches for estimation and prediction, and there is even an R package, **tsiR**, built around time-series susceptible–infected–recovered methods for childhood infections like measles. If your ML model is unstable because labels are sparse, a mechanistic model can be more robust and more scientifically defensible. citeturn1search14turn1search22

The third alternative is a **hybrid model**. Use a mechanistic or rules-based risk score as one input into an ML model, rather than asking ML to learn outbreak dynamics from scratch. This often performs better when the label set is small but expert structure is strong. In measles, that structure includes high transmissibility, the importance of immunity gaps, dry-season and mixing-related seasonality, and the role of population movement. citeturn33search0turn33search3turn20search0

In other words, if the final case-count forecast is not very accurate, you can still produce a very strong graduation contribution by positioning the system as a **woreda-level measles early-warning and risk stratification platform**. In the Ethiopian context, that is not a fallback; it is probably the most realistic and useful public-health product you can build with current public data. citeturn35view0turn34view0

## Open questions and limitations

A few limitations should be stated openly in the project and in the paper. I could verify strong public measles data for **Amhara**, but I could not verify a simple, fully open **national Ethiopia woreda measles line list**. Some important systems, such as raw DHS downloads and Ethiopia’s DHIS2, involve registration or permission, and some humanitarian/conflict sources may require account access or download agreements even when their existence and metadata are public. citeturn29view0turn13search19turn31search15

The second limitation is **boundary inconsistency**. Different Ethiopia sources work with different woreda counts and vintages, and the published district immunization study had to solve non-standardized woreda names across DHIS2, WorldPop, CSA, and boundary files. A serious harmonization layer is therefore essential from the beginning of the project. citeturn36search16turn29view1

The third limitation is **surveillance incompleteness under crisis conditions**. WHO explicitly notes that conflict, under-reporting, weak active surveillance, transport barriers, and access problems all affect measles detection and reporting in Ethiopia. That means model outputs should be interpreted as predictions from imperfect surveillance, not absolute truth. The best long-term solution is exactly the one already built into your project plan: your own platform should gradually create better local labels and better operational covariates over time. citeturn35view0
